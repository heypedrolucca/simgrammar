# Chapter 8. Where AI lands

You start with four tiles: water, fire, wind, and earth. Drag one onto another and a new tile appears with a name and an emoji. Drag that onto something else. Most pairs you try have been tried before by someone, somewhere, and the answer comes back from a database that every player of [[infinite-craft|Infinite Craft]] shares, so you get the same result anyone else would.

After a while you combine two things nobody has combined. This time the database has no entry, and the game asks a language model: a Llama model writes a name for the result, at most twenty tokens long, and picks an emoji. The game stores the answer, and your screen marks the new tile as a First Discovery. From then on every player who drops those two tiles together gets your result [@src-wikipedia-infinite-craft].

Infinite Craft is the clearest case in the dataset of a model deciding a game rule in a shipped game, and it works because the decision is made once. The technique record, LLM-adjudicated state transitions, describes the cache as turning a stochastic model into a growing deterministic rule table, with cost bounded by the number of new pairs.{{assert:field.llm-rule-adjudication.determinism_impact == mitigable}} It also helps that the mechanic has no right answer. When researchers asked GPT-4 to predict the next state of 31 text games across 76,369 transitions, it got the whole state right at most 59.9 percent of the time, and at most 49.7 percent for changes the environment caused on its own [@src-wang-2024-text-world-simulators]. A combination in Infinite Craft only has to be plausible once and consistent afterwards. A model asked to run a city's water table would have to be right.

## Seven ways to change a mechanic

Every technique in the dataset is linked to the mechanics it changes by an `affects` edge, and each edge records one of seven effects, each with a shipped or prototype example in the records:

- **Replaces substrate**: keeps the mechanic's role in play and swaps how it works, as when inZOI's small model picks a Zoi's next action in place of a scoring formula.
- **Deepens**: more fidelity, scale, or variety, as when Gran Turismo Sophy drives the same tire and suspension model the player does at champion level.
- **Enables new form**: a version that was not possible before, as when a PUBG Ally teammate takes spoken orders mid-match.
- **Cuts authoring cost**: cheaper content, as when Genie 3 could render an injected event without per-event art.
- **Explains**: makes the mechanic's state or causes legible to the player.
- **Tunes**: automates balancing and testing, as agent playtesting did for The Sims Mobile's careers.
- **Accelerates**: the same output for less compute, as frame generation raises displayed frame rates in flight and driving sims.

Across {{stat:n_affects_edges}} edges, deepening is the most common effect, at {{stat:n_effect_deepens}} edges, and explaining is the rarest, at {{stat:n_effect_explains}}.{{assert:top_effect == Deepens}}{{assert:rarest_effect == Explains}} Only {{stat:n_explains_techniques}} techniques explain anything. Three of the explaining edges come from one prototype, language-model explanation of state, which turns an agent's thought log into a short account of why it acted, condenses a long chain of modifiers into a ranked list of causes, or annotates a graph with the events that moved it.{{assert:field.llm-explanation-of-state.maturity == prototype}} {{stat:n_effect_explains_shipped}} explaining edge comes from a technique that has shipped: generative agents keep their memories, plans, and reflections as text, so a player or designer can read them or ask the character why it did something.{{assert:n_effect_explains_shipped == 1}}

The imbalance follows research effort. Generating content and replacing substrates attract most of the work, while explaining a running simulation to the person playing it attracts little, although the parts it needs (retrieval over a structured log, constrained generation, summarization) all exist. Chapter 5 described legibility as the cost that deep systems pay, and the dataset finds few techniques aimed at paying it.

The games show what explanation looks like when it is built by hand. RimWorld lists every thought behind a colonist's mood with its value and expiry. Paradox's grand strategy games break every derived number into nested tooltips of named modifiers (chapter 5).{{assert:derived.causal-tooltips.prevalence <= 5}} Dwarf Fortress keeps each dwarf's stress as a hidden number between minus and plus a million, and the player sees the tantrum without the account that produced it. A model that reads the simulation's own modifier lists and event log could give every game the Paradox treatment without the years of interface work, if it narrates what the simulation computed instead of guessing. The text-world results above are the reason for that condition: a model asked to predict dynamics gets them wrong about half the time, so the simulation has to compute the cause and the model has to report it. Chapter 10 returns to this as the highest-ranked opportunity in the dataset, a why-query over the simulation's log.{{assert:top_opportunity == Why-query over the simulation log}}

## Where the set comes from

The techniques in the dataset are recent. {{stat:n_technique_since_2023}} of the {{count:technique}} are dated 2023 or later, and the largest category is generative world models, the frame-predicting systems of chapter 7.{{assert:n_technique_since_2023 > 20}}

{{table:techniques-by-category}}

The set was built by looking for dated results that change a named mechanic, which probably favours fields that publish often and name their applications to games. Language-model agents, world models, and neural rendering publish constantly and point at games directly. Operations research on scheduling, or econometrics on markets, rarely mentions a game, so a method that could balance an economy enters only when someone has applied it to one. Part of the pattern below is a property of the field, and part is a property of the search that built the set.

## Counting exposure

To compare families of mechanics, the dataset sums each mechanic's edges into a *technique exposure*. Each edge counts for more when the technique has shipped than when it is a research result, and for more when it replaces or enables a mechanic than when it only tunes or speeds it up; the weights are written down in the rubrics file. A family's exposure is the sum over its mechanics. The measure says where the set of techniques points, weighted by how close each one is to a game. It does not say what will happen next. A mechanic with no edge is not immune to AI; it means nobody in the set has published a dated result that names it. General-purpose models can write mod code or romance dialogue today, and the dataset leaves those uses out until someone ships or publishes one for a named mechanic. An exposure of zero is a statement about the evidence, and the evidence can change with a single pull request that adds a technique.

{{viz:technique-flows}}

In total, exposure concentrates in four families: narrative and generation, agents and behavior, control and automation, and physics and materials.{{assert:top_exposed_family == Narrative & generation}}{{assert:rank.family-summary.agents-behavior <= 4}}{{assert:rank.family-summary.control-automation <= 4}}{{assert:rank.family-summary.physics-materials <= 4}} These are the families where content is expensive to write (events, dialogue, characters) or behavior is expensive to compute (bodies, vehicles, crowds).

## What the techniques would change

The edges into these families describe specific changes, and most of them are proposals rather than products.

In narrative, the change is from choosing to writing. A storyteller director picks incidents from an authored catalog. Triggered event chains, the machinery behind Crusader Kings III's stories, fire authored templates when conditions hold and schedule follow-ups days or months later, so a rival's illness can lead to a succession dispute and then to a war. A language-model game master would write the event for the exact state of the game, check it against the setting's rules, and narrate it.{{assert:field.llm-game-master.maturity == shipped-niche}} The trade is visible in both failure lists: authored chains repeat once players exhaust the pool, and surveyed language-model game masters invent quests that do not exist in the game [@src-gallotta-2024-llms-games-survey].

In agents, the change is from scoring to asking. Most characters in the catalog pick their next action with a utility scorer: every candidate action gets a score from the character's needs, distances, personality, and context, and the game takes the best one or draws among the top few. The Sims picks the action whose object advertises the largest gain in happiness. Generative agents replace the scorer with a prompt that holds the current step of the day's plan, what the character perceives, and the memories retrieved for the moment; they replace numeric relationship values with remembered interactions; and they replace authored routines with plans each character writes for itself. The research route that keeps most of an existing engine is constrained decoding, which lets a language model choose only among the same enumerated actions the scorer would have ranked, so the game's actions, animations, and rules stay as they were.{{assert:field.constrained-decoding.maturity == research}}

In control, the change is from menus to speech and demonstration. PUBG Ally takes spoken orders about movement, looting, and strategy during a match. Research agents that play commercial 3D games through the screen and a virtual keyboard and mouse follow language instructions and explain what they intend to do [@src-deepmind-2025-sima-2-blog], and agents in the Voyager line write, test, and keep new routines in a growing library of skills [@src-wang-2023-voyager].{{assert:field.generalist-game-agents.maturity == research}}{{assert:field.voyager-skill-libraries.maturity == research}}

In physics, almost every proposal replaces the engine with learned video prediction. Genie, WHAMM, and the open interactive video models render collisions, gravity, and car handling from what they learned watching footage, with no conservation laws and no stored geometry. DIAMOND's Counter-Strike model let the player jump again and again in mid-air, which the real game forbids [@src-alonso-2024-diamond]. The physics family's exposure is high because these models touch rigid bodies, vehicles, and destruction all at once, and every one of those edges comes from a prototype or a research result.{{assert:field.open-interactive-video-models.maturity == prototype}}{{assert:field.physical-world-foundation-models.maturity == research}}

Divided by the number of mechanics in each family, the order changes. {{stat:top_mean_exposed_family}} still leads and world generation comes second, while agents and physics drop out of the top three: their totals partly reflected how many mechanics they hold.{{assert:top_mean_exposed_family == Narrative & generation}}{{assert:rank.family-mean-exposure.world-generation <= 2}}{{assert:rank.family-mean-exposure.agents-behavior > 3}}{{assert:rank.family-mean-exposure.physics-materials > 3}}

## The economic core

At the other end sit the families most simulation games are built from. Per mechanic, resources, logistics, economy, and population all fall in the lower half of the families, and resources and logistics sit near the bottom.{{assert:rank.family-mean-exposure.resources-production >= 16}}{{assert:rank.family-mean-exposure.logistics-networks >= 16}}{{assert:rank.family-mean-exposure.economy-finance > 10}}{{assert:rank.family-mean-exposure.population-lineage > 10}} Operation and procedure, the family of checklists, surface tools, and repair, has no technique attached at all.{{assert:least_exposed_family == Operation & procedure}}{{assert:n_family_zero_exposure_operation_procedure == n_family_mechanics_operation_procedure}}

Resources and production shows the pattern most plainly. It is the most used family in the catalog,{{assert:most_used_family == Resources & production}} and of its {{stat:n_family_mechanics_resources_production}} mechanics, {{stat:n_family_zero_exposure_resources_production}} have no technique in the set pointed at them.{{assert:n_family_zero_exposure_resources_production >= 10}} The two that do are hand crafting, which Infinite Craft's cached combinations turn into an open-ended recipe space, and machine boosting, where a research method searches module layouts for the dominant configurations that flatten a player's choices.{{assert:derived.hand-crafting.technique_exposure > 0}}{{assert:derived.machine-boosting.technique_exposure > 0}} Production chains, extraction, storage, spoilage, crops, and power have nothing.{{assert:derived.production-chain.technique_exposure == 0}}{{assert:derived.resource-extraction.technique_exposure == 0}}{{assert:derived.storage-capacity.technique_exposure == 0}}{{assert:derived.spoilage.technique_exposure == 0}}{{assert:derived.crop-cultivation.technique_exposure == 0}}{{assert:derived.power-grid.technique_exposure == 0}}

Logistics fares only slightly better. Of its {{stat:n_family_mechanics_logistics_networks}} mechanics, {{stat:n_family_zero_exposure_logistics_networks}} have no technique, and every edge that reaches the family comes from research: mean-field games that would let drivers anticipate congestion instead of reacting to it, GPU simulation that points at agent-level traffic at much larger counts, and calibration methods that fit a traffic model to observed counts.{{assert:field.mean-field-games.maturity == research}}{{assert:field.massively-parallel-simulation.maturity == research}}{{assert:field.simulation-based-inference.maturity == research}} Economy and finance has {{stat:n_family_mechanics_economy_finance}} mechanics, and {{stat:n_family_zero_exposure_economy_finance}} of them have no technique. The one shipped technique with an edge into the family is tree search, which can let rival firms choose prices and expansions by rolling the market forward.{{assert:field.monte-carlo-tree-search.maturity == shipped-niche}}

Chapter 1 noted that most of the ten most used mechanics have no technique attached. Across the whole vocabulary, {{stat:n_mechanics_zero_exposure}} mechanics are affected by no technique in the set, and they account for {{stat:pct_uses_on_zero_exposure}} percent of every game-to-mechanic use in the catalog.{{assert:pct_uses_on_zero_exposure >= 40}}

The production chain's own record lists the problems a technique could address. "A single optimal ratio per product, once solved, turns play into copying calculator output": a design problem that search over recipes and layouts could expose before release. Upstream starvation that players blame on the wrong building is an explanation problem, since the simulation already knows which stage is starving and why; chapter 10 opens with it. Victoria 3 sets each good's price from the balance of buy and sell orders in each market, and the record for supply-demand pricing lists boom-and-bust cycles "that players read as random" among its failure modes, another case where the simulation computes the cause and does not show it.{{assert:derived.supply-demand-pricing.technique_exposure < 0.5}}

Part of this is sensible. A production chain is cheap to compute and, by the rater's scores, about as easy to read as the average mechanic; there is less for a learned model to fix. Part of it is a gap. Economies are where simulation games break: a price or loan loophole turns the economy into an exploit, recipe graphs have to be authored and balanced by hand, and scenarios have to be tested against strategies no designer tried. Those are tuning and authoring problems, and tuning methods exist. Agent-based playtesting has shipped in production pipelines, and search that keeps the best design for each niche of a space is an established research method.{{assert:field.automated-playtesting.maturity == shipped-niche}}{{assert:field.quality-diversity-search.maturity == research}}

Generative content and procedural terrain lead the ranking, because many techniques generate content or worlds.{{assert:rank.most-exposed-mechanics.generative-content <= 2}}{{assert:rank.most-exposed-mechanics.procedural-terrain <= 2}} Utility action selection, the scoring that picks a character's next action from its needs, comes third:{{assert:rank.most-exposed-mechanics.utility-action-selection == 3}} language models, planners, and learned policies all offer to replace it, and inZOI's Smart Zois are the shipped case, a small local model choosing among the game's own actions. The scorer it would replace runs the characters of {{derived:utility-action-selection.prevalence}} catalog games, from Theme Park in 1994 through The Sims and Theme Hospital to RimWorld and Prison Architect.{{assert:derived.utility-action-selection.earliest_game == theme-park}} Rigid-body physics and direct avatar control follow,{{assert:rank.most-exposed-mechanics.rigid-body-physics <= 6}}{{assert:rank.most-exposed-mechanics.direct-avatar <= 6}} exposed mainly to frame-generating world models such as Genie, which replace both with learned prediction and carry over the consistency limits chapter 7 described.

World generation shows the same split inside one family. Procedural terrain is affected by {{derived:procedural-terrain.technique_count}} techniques: satellite reconstruction rebuilt the planet for Microsoft Flight Simulator, learned generators trained on example maps would replace hand-written noise and placement rules, and world models generate a scene from a prompt and forget it a minute later.{{assert:derived.procedural-terrain.technique_count >= 5}} Simulated history, the mechanic behind Dwarf Fortress's world generation, has none. Generative content is used by {{derived:generative-content.prevalence}} catalog games, almost all of them AI-native titles, and is affected by {{derived:generative-content.technique_count}} techniques.{{assert:derived.generative-content.prevalence < 10}} The most exposed mechanic in the set is one of the least used.

## Judgment against evidence

Every mechanic also carries a judged score for AI leverage: how much, in the rater's reading, current techniques could change it. AI leverage scores and exposure correlate at ρ = {{stat:rho_ai_leverage_exposure}}.{{assert:rho_ai_leverage_exposure >= 0.3}} That number is a consistency check and not independent agreement. The same model rater scored AI leverage, wrote the `affects` edges, and judged each technique's maturity, so a correlation between them shows the rater was consistent with itself. A second, calibrated rater is the remedy, and chapter 13 describes how one is being added.

## Exercise: aim a technique

Pick one mechanic from the table of gaps above.

1. Write down the state it owns and the rule that updates it, from its record.
2. Choose one effect from the seven: replace, deepen, enable, cut authoring cost, explain, tune, or accelerate. Pick the one that would matter most to a player.
3. Name the smallest technique that could do it, and say whether its output would enter authoritative state or stay in presentation.
4. Describe the experiment that would show it worked, with a number a stranger could check.

Step 3 decides most of the risk. Chapter 9 turns from techniques to the mechanics themselves and looks for pairs of common parts that almost never appear in the same game.
