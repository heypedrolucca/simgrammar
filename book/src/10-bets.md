# Chapter 10. Bets

Science output has halved, and the production graph says so without saying why. You open it and find the drop in electronic circuits, so you build four more circuit assemblers and connect them. Output stays where it was. The new assemblers sit with empty input slots. Twenty minutes later you trace the problem upstream by hand: the copper cable assemblers are starved, because the copper smelters share a belt with iron, and the iron side fills the lanes first. The new circuit assemblers had been waiting on cable the whole time.

The dataset's record for production chains lists this among the mechanic's failure modes: starvation far upstream is hard to diagnose without flow statistics, so players blame the wrong building. It also lists the reason the chase never ends: the slowest stage sets throughput, so the bottleneck moves each time you fix one. [[factorio|Factorio]] gives you better instruments than most games, per-item production and consumption graphs and a panel for the power network, and when power runs short the game shares it evenly, so every machine on the grid slows at once and the symptom shows up everywhere except at its cause.

A tool that answered the question directly would not need a new kind of AI. Perturb the factory: add a little capacity to each machine, belt, and buffer in turn, and measure how much the selected output rises. The machine whose extra capacity raises output most is the binding constraint. A short generated sentence can then name it, with a link that jumps the camera to the spot: "circuits are limited by copper cable, which is starved because the smelter column shares a belt with iron." That is the design sketch of one opportunity in the dataset, the bottleneck explainer. Production chains are among the most widely used mechanics in the catalog, and no technique in the dataset is linked to them.{{assert:derived.production-chain.prevalence_rank <= 10}}{{assert:derived.production-chain.technique_exposure == 0}}

This chapter is about bets of that kind: specific designs that combine mechanics the catalog already has with techniques the dataset records, each with an experiment that would test it and a dated prediction that can come out wrong.

## What a bet contains

The dataset holds {{count:opportunity}} opportunities. Each one is a record with the same fields, and the fields make it testable.

The record names the mechanics it reworks and the techniques it applies, so the bet sits on the same graph as everything else in the book. It names precedent games that partly demonstrate it; for the bottleneck explainer they are Factorio, Satisfactory, Anno 1800, and Oxygen Not Included, the games whose players already hunt bottlenecks by hand. It states a thesis, a design sketch, and the risks the authors of the record saw.

Then come the two fields that matter most. The validation experiment says what a small team would build to test the idea and what result would count as success. For the bottleneck explainer: implement sensitivity analysis over a factory prototype's production graph, inject a bottleneck, and see whether players fix it faster with the bottleneck view than without; success is fixes 50 percent faster, with no drop in satisfaction among experienced players when the view is optional. The falsifiable signal predicts something about the world by a date: a factory or city-building game will ship a feature that names the binding constraint in the player's network by testing the simulation, not by displaying static ratios, by 30 June 2028, in a title with at least 1,000 Steam user reviews, with patch notes or a developer blog saying the feature re-runs or perturbs the network.{{assert:field.bottleneck-explainer.falsifiable_signal.resolve_by == 2028-06-30}} Chapter 12 follows those predictions.

Each record is scored from 1 to 5 on three anchored scales: impact, feasibility, and novelty. Priority is their weighted geometric mean, with impact weighted at 0.45, feasibility at 0.35, and novelty at 0.20. A geometric mean punishes a low score on any axis, so a brilliant idea nobody can build and a buildable idea nobody needs both sink. ## The top of the list

The highest-ranked opportunity is {{stat:top_opportunity}}.{{assert:top_opportunity == Why-query over the simulation log}}{{assert:rank.opportunities-ranked.causal-why-query == 1}} Its thesis starts from the constraint chapter 5 described: players abandon systems they cannot diagnose, so designers cap how much the systems touch each other. Overlays and causal tooltips explain current values: this district's land value is low because of the factory next door. They do not explain histories. "Why did the hospital close?" is a question about a chain of events over months of game time, and no tooltip answers it.

The proposal is to record the simulation's events with their causes, then let a language model retrieve the chain behind any outcome and narrate it, with every sentence linked to a logged event the player can jump to on the timeline. A right-click "why?" on any building, person, or indicator returns three to six sentences and a small causal graph. An answer that cannot be grounded in the log says so. The constraint on the output format does the safety work: the model may only make claims it can point at.

The thesis rests on an observation about engines. Many simulation games already keep an event log for replays and debugging, and the proposal turns that log into something players see. [[dwarf-fortress|Dwarf Fortress]], [[victoria-3|Victoria 3]], [[rimworld|RimWorld]], and [[cities-skylines-2|Cities: Skylines II]] are listed as precedents because each exposes some causal state already. The record also lists what could go wrong: fluent answers that are not grounded teach players wrong models of the system; shipped event logs often lack cause links, and adding them is real engineering; and an explanation layer can make optimal play too easy. The experiment is designed around the first risk. Give two groups the same failing save, give one group the why-query, and measure time to a correct diagnosis and the share of answer sentences whose cited events actually support them, judged by people. Success is diagnosis 40 percent faster with at least 95 percent of sentences supported.

Second on the list is a branch preview of policy futures.{{assert:rank.opportunities-ranked.branch-preview-forecasts == 2}} Forecasts in shipped games are thin: a countdown, a weather icon, a predicted change in approval. The proposal forks the live game state in the background before a decision, 32 to 256 copies with different random seeds, fast-forwards each one an in-game year on spare cores or through a learned surrogate, and shows the player fan charts of the key indicators and replays of the two futures that differ most. Using the preview costs an in-game resource so that it stays a decision. A deterministic simulation can fork from any tick, which means the preview runs the game's real rules instead of a hand-built predictor. The validation target is concrete: 64 branches previewed in under five seconds on a mid-range PC, and a measurable drop in decision regret without lower engagement.

Third, tied with two others, is the event-sourced simulation runtime: an engine layer that records every state change, including those a model decides, as an append-only log with inputs, outputs, and causes.{{assert:rank.opportunities-ranked.event-sourced-sim-runtime <= 5}} It is infrastructure, and chapter 11 explains why so many of the other bets depend on it. The why-query reads its log. The branch preview forks from it. A replay or a multiplayer client reads logged model outputs instead of calling the model again.

## A colonist with a memory

Another bet tied at third returns to the cook from chapter 1. In RimWorld, as in The Sims, almost every decision an agent makes is routine: eat, sleep, haul, cook. Rule-based utility scoring handles those cheaply and legibly. The memorable moments are rare: a death in the family, a public insult, a mental break, a first meeting. The salience-gated agent proposal keeps the utility loop for every tick and calls a language model only when an event crosses a salience score.{{assert:rank.opportunities-ranked.salience-gated-llm-agents <= 5}}{{assert:field.salience-gated-llm-agents.horizon == now}}

The call is narrow. The colonist's traits, recent memories, and the event go in. What comes out must parse against a schema: a thought with a mood value and a duration, an optional change to a relationship, an optional one-line spoken reaction, an optional new goal. The player sees the result as an ordinary thought in the needs tab, the same list that explained the cook's breakdown, except that the line could now name her son and mention the argument they had the week before he died. Nothing in the game's rules changes. The memory system gains entries that no writer had to author.

The experiment caps the model at one call per colonist per in-game hour and compares a pure-utility colony of 20 with a salience-gated one in blind playtests. It measures believability and attachment on a seven-point scale, tokens per player-hour, and state contradictions per hour, such as a colonist mourning someone who is alive. Success needs all of them: a gain of at least one point, fewer than 2,000 tokens per player-hour, and fewer than one contradiction an hour. The record dates the opportunity's feasibility to 2025, when small local models made per-colonist calls affordable, and constrained decoding is what lets the result be written back into game state safely.

Language modding, tied at third, applies the same discipline to the players who extend games.{{assert:rank.opportunities-ranked.language-modding <= 5}} Modding keeps simulation games alive for decades, and writing a mod takes programming skill and knowledge of the engine. The proposal is an in-game workshop where a player describes a change in a sentence ("wolves avoid fire", "trains prefer cheaper routes"), a model proposes a diff to data and scripts against the documented modding API, and agents run a headless test scenario to check for crashes and for the intended effect before the mod can be shared. The description, the code, and the test results travel together. The experiment takes a hundred real requests from a modding community and counts how many compile, pass the scenario, and are judged by the person who asked to do what they asked; success is 60 percent.

## The pattern behind the list

Read the top of the table together and the same design choice keeps appearing. None of the leading bets asks a learned model to become the simulation. The authoritative state stays symbolic, typed, and deterministic, and models sit around it: they read it and explain it, as in the why-query and the bottleneck explainer; they propose changes that a validator accepts or rejects, as in language modding and salience-gated agents; or they decorate it, writing the line a colonist says without deciding whether she lives.

The dataset's synthesis puts the pattern as five principles, and each opportunity applies at least one:

- **Keep the simulation symbolic and let models read, explain, decorate, and propose** through validated interfaces.
- **Spend inference on salience.** Most ticks need no model; calls at moments that matter, cached and logged, buy believability at bounded cost.
- **Explain before you generate.** Comprehension limits how much coupling players can use (chapter 5), and few techniques target explanation (chapter 8).
- **Generate offline and verify with agents.** Content, recipes, scenarios, mods, and balance can be produced at design time and checked by automated play before any player sees them.
- **Log every model call as an event,** which restores replay, multiplayer sync, and bug reports, and gives explanation and forecasting their raw material.

The principles follow from the evidence in the earlier chapters. Chapter 7 found that what has shipped mostly stays at the edge of simulation state, and chapter 8 found that the economic core of the genre has almost no technique pointed at it.{{assert:n_technique_shipped_mainstream <= 2}}{{assert:rank.family-mean-exposure.resources-production >= 16}}{{assert:rank.family-mean-exposure.logistics-networks >= 16}} Bets that keep the core symbolic can ship on techniques that already work. Bets that replace the core with learned prediction inherit the failure modes chapter 11 describes.

## Further down

Some bets fill the vacant lots of chapter 9. Building placement that triggers story events ranks seventh.{{assert:rank.opportunities-ranked.placement-that-tells-stories <= 10}} Factories whose workers age, form families, and are replaced, and research driven by named scientists in place of a tech tree, sit lower; each pairs two common mechanics that almost never meet.

Others are business models or infrastructure more than features. Local-first AI simulation games ship small language models inside the game so that AI features run offline with no inference bill per player-hour, which fits the one-time purchase most simulation games are sold on and the very long sessions people play them for.{{assert:field.local-first-ai-sims.opportunity_type == business-model}} Its record cites [[inzoi|inZOI]]'s on-device characters as a precedent.

At the bottom of the feasibility scale are the long shots, each scored 5 for novelty and 2 for feasibility, with a three-to-five-year horizon.{{assert:field.llm-society-terrarium.feasibility == 2}}{{assert:field.mechanic-discovery-search.feasibility == 2}}{{assert:field.symbolic-sim-neural-render.feasibility == 2}} A language-agent society terrarium makes watching a society of language-model agents the game: the player picks a map and founding traits, seeds 50 to 500 agents, intervenes rarely with a drought or a prophet, and reads the chronicle. Symbolic simulation with neural rendering keeps a city builder's state as a tile grid and lets a generative model draw it, so a player could restyle a city as watercolor without new art while saves, replays, and inspection tools read the grid. ## Who should build what

The bets sort by who is in a position to make them. A small studio has the ones with feasibility of 4 or 5 and a horizon of now: a legibility layer over an existing simulation, schema-constrained events, salience-gated agents on a local model, offline balancing. Each can ship as a feature of a conventional design. An engine or middleware company has the infrastructure: event-sourced runtimes that log model calls, constrained interfaces from language to game actions, headless simulation for testing agents, and libraries of calibrated surrogates for weather and materials. A research group has the open problems: rendering symbolic state with guarantees that the picture matches it, learned material models that conserve mass and energy, automated discovery of mechanics, and ways to measure whether an explanation is faithful. Organizations outside games can use simulation games as instruments; one bet proposes factory, colony, and economy sims as long-horizon benchmarks for AI agents, with studios as the providers of the environments.

## Exercise: write a bet

Take a design idea you have been carrying around and write it as an opportunity record.

1. Name the mechanics it reworks, using the catalog's names, and the technique it depends on. If no technique in the dataset fits, name the published result you would cite.
2. Name two or three games that partly demonstrate it today.
3. Write the validation experiment: what you would build in a month, who would play it, what you would measure, and the number that would count as success.
4. Write a prediction with a date and criteria a stranger could check from public evidence: which kind of game, how many Steam reviews or which platforms, and what a store page or patch note would have to say. Then write down what you would conclude if the date passed with nothing shipped.

Most ideas get harder to state at step 3. The bottleneck explainer's record handles it with a number a playtest can miss: fixes 50 percent faster.
